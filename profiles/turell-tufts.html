Biography

“Turell , b. 1770; d. June 9, 1842.”

Charles Brooks, History of the Town of Medford (1855), Register of Families, p. 546. Page scan · Text (Perseus), among the children of Dr. Simon Tufts, jun., by his second wife, Elizabeth Hall

Turell Tufts was the eldest son of the second marriage of Dr. Simon Tufts (1727–1786). He was named for the Rev. Ebenezer Turell, Medford’s second minister, whose portrait he later left to the First Parish. His uncle Dr. Cotton Tufts described him to John Adams in 1798 as a man “bred in the Mercantile Line” who had been in business at Hartford, Connecticut, and had lost heavily through Georgia land speculation and French depredations. He recommended him as well qualified for a consulship.

“Turell Tufts, a Son of my Brother Dr. Tufts decd.”

Cotton Tufts to John Adams, June 1798, Adams Family Correspondence, vol. 13 (Adams Papers document ADMS-04-13-02-0057). MHS digital edition

“I have made Turell Tufts a Consul at Surinam.”

John Adams to Abigail Adams, 28 January 1799. MHS, Adams Family Papers

The Adams Papers editors note that Adams nominated him consul to Suriname on 10 January 1799 and that the Senate confirmed him on 14 January. He wrote from Paramaribo as “T. Tufts Consul USA” to Secretary of State James Madison on 28 January 1808, reporting the embargo of American vessels at Barbados (Papers of James Madison, Secretary of State Series; original in National Archives, RG 59, Consular Despatches, Paramaribo). His younger brother Hall Tufts died at Surinam in 1801 (Brooks, p. 546).

Back in Medford he became one of the town’s leading citizens. When General Lafayette visited, it was Turell Tufts, as chairman of the selectmen, who gave the address of welcome:

“the Selectmen of the town met the General and his cortege ; and they thus addressed him, by Turell Tufts, Esq., their Chairman”

Charles Brooks, History of the Town of Medford (1855), p. 141, in the account of Governor Brooks and Lafayette. Page scan (Internet Archive)

“Turell Tufts was selectman, 1820—1825 and 1827-1828. He served the town as its treasurer in 1827. In 1824 he was fence viewer, on the committee to audit the treasurer’s books, was chosen moderator in absence of Dudley Hall, who was first chosen, and was representative to the General Court.”

Eliza M. Gill, “Turell Tufts and His Family Connections,” Medford Historical Register, vol. XVIII (1915), p. 55. Text (Perseus)

Turell Tufts was selectman, 1820-1825and 1827-1828. He served the town as its treasurer in 1827. In 1824 he was fence viewer, on the committee to audit the treasurer’s books, was chosen moderator in absence of Dudley Hall, who was first chosen, and was representative to the General Court. He had forty-nine votes to Mr. Hall’s fifty-six, and there was a third candidate. The following year, with others, he was on the committee to petition the Legislature in behalf of the new bridge over Charles river from Charlestown to Boston ; again selectman, and on the committee to audit the treasurer’s report.

Transcription of the clipping above: Eliza M. Gill, “Turell Tufts and His Family Connections,” The Medford Historical Register, vol. XVIII (1915), p. 55. View the page scan

He left Medford several bequests: five hundred dollars for roadside shade trees, five hundred dollars to the Medford Social Library, and communion plate and the portrait of Mr. Turell to the church.

“A legacy of five hundred dollars from Turell Tufts , Esq. , was expended, according to his directions, in planting ornamental trees on the roadsides.”

Charles Brooks, History of the Town of Medford (1855), p. 58–59. Text (Perseus)

“Turell Tufts , Esq. , bequeathed to it five hundred dollars, the interest of which must be expended annually for the purchase of valuable books.”

Charles Brooks, History of the Town of Medford (1855), p. 294. Text (Perseus), on the Medford Social Library

“1842, July.--The church received, from the hand of Dudley Hall, a bequest of the late Turell Tufts , Esq. ,--two pieces of plate for the communion-table; and a portrait of the Rev. Mr. Turell , one of the former pastors of this church.”

Charles Brooks, History of the Town of Medford (1855), p. 494. Text (Perseus)

He lived in the old family house at Medford Square, “successively the home of the elder and younger Drs. Simon, and Turell Tufts, Esq.” (“The Tufts Family Residences,” Medford Historical Register, vol. XVIII (1915), p. 67). See Places.

A few old residents there are who remember the old house which was successively the home of the elder and younger Drs. Simon, and Turell Tufts, Esq., at Medford square. This, decrepit with age, was demolished in 1867 and succeeded by the present and soon to be removed building erected by Dr. Weymouth in 1872. When Dr. Weymouth at its completion addressed a company gathered there, he submitted the question of a name for the hall it contained, and suggested that of Tufts as appropriate. Adopted by acclamation, as Tufts hall it has ever been known.

Transcription of the clipping above: “The Tufts Family Residences,” The Medford Historical Register, vol. XVIII (1915), p. 67. View the page scan

Coolidge, working from the Hall family papers, found that Turell and his brother Hall Tufts carried on trade with Surinam; Hall died there on July 19, 1801, and Turell wrote from Surinam in 1808 to Lucy Hall on the death of her husband. (Ruth Dame Coolidge, “Simon Tufts the Third, Merchant of India,” Medford Historical Register XLI, no. 3 (September 1938), no. 4, pp. 61–62)

The Surinam Years and the Return to Medford

Turell Tufts spent long years at Surinam, the Dutch colony on the north coast of South America where his brother Hall Tufts had died in 1801. A letter he wrote from there in November 1808 to his kinsman Dudley Hall, whom he addresses as “Cousin” and whose mother had written to him, was copied into the Hall family papers of the Medford Historical Society and printed by Ruth Dame Coolidge in 1938. He wrote of feeling “banished” from home and longing to see Medford again, and he added a merchant’s postscript on the Embargo of 1807, which had cut off American shipping to the colony:

“Surinam 20 Nov. 1808. My dear Cousin, I thank you for your Letter in answer to mine to your mother. Tho’ it contains subjects for grief and serious reflections, I am gratified in seeing the hand writing & the thoughts of a relation, whose countenance & manners, most probably, I should not now recognise. The length of time I have been banished as it were from my country, upon reviewing it, makes me think that I am growing old: and I am told that my native Town, and the surrounding country are so much improved that I should as little recollect the Scenes of my youthful life as the countenances of those who now inhabit them. … P.S. The Embargo had an immediate effect of making most articles scarce & dear in this Colony. From indirect Sources however, we have been supplied, and tho’ much groaning & murmuring is made at prices, most things are now to be had and prices are falling. … When the Embargo is raised it will be hazardous to send cargoes to these Regions, such will be the glut & consequent loss to adventurers. … Dec 10. 1808. This Vessel has been detained unexpectedly. No American Vessel remains!”

Ruth Dame Coolidge, “Simon Tufts the Third, Merchant of India,” Medford Historical Register XLI, no. 4 (December 1938), p. 62. View the page scan

He later gave up living in Surinam and came home, leaving the business to an agent. Coolidge found a letter from that agent, a man named Nash, dated December 8, 1832, reporting a fire. She also connects Turell’s $500 bequest to the Medford Social Library with the library’s renaming in his honor:

“At some time during the following years Turell Tufts evidently abandoned Surinam and returned to Medford. Perhaps he feared the fate of his brother Hall, whose death in Surinam is noted on the tombstone. The business was entrusted to an agent and occasioned much correspondence. On December 8, 1832, an agent named Nash sent a letter by the brig Sarah Ann, a letter postmarked in Gloucester, December 31, an indication of the length of the passage. There had been a fire and Nash had a chance to settle things up. We do not know how successful Turell was in business, but on his death, in 1842, he left a fund of $500.00 to the town to be used for the purchase of valuable books for the Medford Social Library. So munificent was the gift regarded that the name was changed from Medford Social Library to Medford Tufts Library, in honor of Turell Tufts. The name, however, was eventually changed, by vote of the town, to Medford Public Library.”

Ruth Dame Coolidge, “Simon Tufts the Third, Merchant of India,” Medford Historical Register XLI, no. 4 (December 1938), p. 63. View the page scan

Three years before his death, the printed town report of 1839 still listed him among Medford’s larger taxpayers, those assessed more than one hundred dollars, alongside Dudley Hall and the Medford Distillery:

“… Dudley Hall … the Medford Distillery, Turell Tufts, Peter Brooks, and the Osgood sisters. As these taxes were for more than one hundred dollars and up to over five hundred, as in the case of Thatcher Magoun …”

Wade S. Hervey, ed., “The Medford Town Report of 1839,” Medford Historical Register XLII, no. 1 (March 1939), pp. 1–2. View the page scan

His own amount is not given in the Register’s summary. Note: Pending further work.

His Portrait

A portrait of Turell Tufts belonged to the Medford Historical Society. It was shown at the Society’s Royall House loan exhibition (Medford Historical Register III, “The Royall House Loan Exhibition,” text on Perseus), and Eliza M. Gill wrote in 1915 that it hung on the Society’s library wall, the gift of Dudley C. Hall (Medford Historical Register XVIII, text on Perseus). Coolidge described it in 1938:

“Turell returned from Surinam to live and die in Medford. The Historical Society has his portrait, a shrewd, kindly face, with clear blue eyes.”

Ruth Dame Coolidge, “Simon Tufts the Third, Merchant of India,” Medford Historical Register XLI, no. 4 (December 1938), p. 63. View the page scan

The House in an Early Photograph

An original photograph of the house at High and Forest Streets has come down in the family. It shows the same view later printed in the Medford Historical Register, and it must date from before the house was pulled down in 1867. See Places: Homesteads.

“Uncle Turel’s House” in a Letter of 1839

A family letter written after the Medford town house fire of 27 October 1839 mentions “Uncle Turels House” among the buildings near the blaze. The Register’s editor, printing the letter in 1937, explained the reference:

““Uncle Turels House” was the house of Turell Tufts, son of Simon Tufts, Medford’s Revolutionary doctor, friend of Isaac Royall. This old Tufts family homestead was on the corner of Forest and High Streets and was torn down in 1867. Turell Tufts himself died three years after this letter, leaving $500.00 to the town as a perpetual trust, the income to be expended annually for valuable books for the Medford Social Library, founded in 1825.”

“A Medford Letter of 1839,” Medford Historical Register XL (December 1937), p. 60. View the page scan

Tufts association: the homestead of Dr. Simon Tufts and his son Turell Tufts, at Forest and High Streets, Medford (“A Medford Letter of 1839,” Medford Historical Register XL (December 1937), p. 60. View the page scan).

Service Record

Town: Medford, Mass. · Dates: 1770–1842

No military service. Civil offices: U.S. consul at Paramaribo, Surinam (nominated 10 Jan. 1799); Medford selectman 1820–25 and 1827–28, town treasurer 1827, and representative to the General Court in 1824 (Gill, p. 55).

Family Connection

“The children by this union were Turell , born 1770, died 1842, unmarried; Cotton, born 1772, died 1835, was insane for forty-four years; Hall , born 1775, died 1801, at Surinam ; Hepsibah, born 1777; Stephen, born 1779, died young.”

Eliza M. Gill, “Turell Tufts and His Family Connections,” Medford Historical Register, vol. XVIII (1915), p. 54. Text (Perseus), on the children of Dr. Simon Tufts, Jr. and Elizabeth Hall

Turell Tufts, who made the speech of welcome to Lafayette, belonged to a family prominent in the business and social life of the town for many years. On the maternal side he was the fifth generation from John Hall, the first of this family to establish a home in Medford; and in what was once called the Old Garrison House, descendants of the eighth and ninth generation are living today, while the tenth generation is also represented in the city. In this same house Lucy Tufts, after her marriage to Benjamin Hall, made her early home. Turell Tufts on the paternal side was fifth in the line of descent from Peter Tufts of Malden, whose son, Captain Peter …

Father: Dr. Simon Tufts (1727–1786). Mother: Elizabeth Hall (born May 15, 1743), daughter of Stephen Hall; she married secondly, on July 12, 1795, Capt. Duncan Ingraham of Concord, and died in 1830 aged 87 (Gill, pp. 54, 56). Grandfather: Dr. Simon Tufts (1700–1747). Uncle: Dr. Cotton Tufts. Half-brother: Simon Tufts (1750–1801) (Brooks, p. 546, lists “Simon, b. 1750” as the first son by Lucy Dudley; Gill, p. 54, says he died at the Cape of Good Hope in 1802).

Relatives with profiles on this site: father; grandfather; uncle; half-brother Simon; ancestor Peter Tufts the immigrant (Gill, p. 54: “fifth in the line of descent from Peter Tufts of Malden”); brother Hall Tufts (1775–1801).

The Minister’s Bequest

The portrait he left to the church had come to him as a child under the will of the Rev. Ebenezer Turell, who died in 1778.

“I give to little Turell Tufts . . . that my shadow may remain”

Medford Historical Register, vol. XXIV, no. 3 (Sept. 1921), “Season of 1920–1921,” pp. 66–67. Internet Archive

… reports were made, and officers elected for the ensuing year. The February meeting was “An Evening with Parson Turell.” Mr. Remele read selections from Brooks’ History relating to him. Mr. Mann read the will of the old minister, having made copy of the same at the Probate office. At the “Item — I give to little Turell Tufts . . . that my shadow may remain” the portrait of Ebenezer Turell thus bequeathed was displayed by Mr. Fiske, who had procured it from the First Parish Church for the occasion. At the “item, I give to Simon Tufts my watch” a silver watch with chain and seal was passed around for inspection. This watch (doubtless similar to Mr. Turell’s) had just been given to the Society, and was that of Dr. Daniel Osgood, brother of Rev. David Osgood, Mr. Turell’s colleague and successor.
