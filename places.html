Winter Hill

Tufts association: Farm and home of Peter Tufts of Winter Hill (1728–1791) and Anne Adams Tufts, where Paul Revere roused him on April 19, 1775. (Alvin Lincoln Jones, Under Colonial Roofs (1894), p. 129)

“At the outbreak of the Revolution, Peter Tufts lived at the top of Winter Hill in a little old house, which stood until a few years ago. Here he was soundly sleeping when Paul Revere came thundering up to the door, calling on the people to rouse and arm themselves, for the Regulars were coming.”

Alvin Lincoln Jones, Under Colonial Roofs (1894), p. 129 · page scan

“The tablet rests upon the historic door stone of the Anne Adams Tufts homestead and was recently presented to the chapter.”

Jay Franklin Tufts, Tufts Family History 1633–1963 (secondary source), p. 28, quoting the Boston Journal, June 22, 1909, on the tablet unveiled in Paul Revere Park, Winter Hill, Somerville · page scan

The tablet reads in part: “This was the door-stone of her home which stood about 120 feet south-west of this spot. [The middle digit is worn on the bronze and appears to be 2; some transcriptions read 100.]” Full transcription on Anne Adams Tufts.

Tufts Library, Weymouth

Tufts association: Public library founded by bequests of Quincy Tufts (1791–1872) and Susannah Tufts (1797–1877), grandchildren of Dr. Cotton Tufts. (Town of Weymouth, “Library History” (Tufts Library founding). Town page; Weymouth Public Libraries, Tufts / Smith / Adams Family Collection finding aid (Collection ID WCT002; prepared C. G. Swank, June 2016). Finding aid (PDF))

“The Tufts Library was founded in 1879, when the Town of Weymouth received bequests of money and land from Quincy Tufts and his sister Susannah, grandchildren of Weymouth physician, Dr. Cotton Tufts, for the establishment of a public library. Part of the bequest included two wooden buildings at the corner of Washington and Commercial Streets in Weymouth Landing. The lower floor of these buildings was set up as the library.”

Town of Weymouth, “Library History” (Tufts Library founding). Town page

“On January 1, 1880, with Miss Caroline A. Blanchard as the first librarian, the Tufts Library opened its doors to the public. The library had over 2,300 volumes, and within a year, it had almost as many borrowers as books.”

Town of Weymouth, “Library History” (Tufts Library founding). Town page

A purpose-built brick library on the Tufts land opened October 6, 1892; a later main library at Weston Park opened in 1965 (same town history). Profiles: Quincy Tufts, Susannah Tufts, Dr. Cotton Tufts.

Tufts House, Farmington, Maine (1810)

“Built by Francis Tufts, a brickmaker,” the Federal-style brick house was made from “clay Tufts took from the banks of the nearby Sandy River.” The Maine Historic Preservation Commission adds that Tufts came from Medford with his family in 1775 and built the first mills at Farmington Falls. It was listed on the National Register in 1979. (Maine Historic Preservation Commission)

Tufts association: Built in 1810 and lived in by Rev. Francis Tufts (1744–1833) of Medford, who made its bricks himself; a proprietor of Farmington and builder of its first mills. (National Register nomination, Tufts House, NRIS 79000130)

The 1979 National Register nomination names the builder and his story: “Francis Tufts, born in 1744 in Medford, Massachusetts, came originally to the Farmington area in 1780 from Nobleboro where he had moved with his family in 1775.” He had built a frame house nearby in 1791, replacing a log dwelling, and the bricks of the 1810 house “were manufactured by the builder, a professional brickmaker among other accomplishments, from clay taken from the banks of the Sandy River.” He was “one of three men who negotiated the purchase of the township from the Massachusetts General Court in 1790,” built and owned the first mills at Farmington Falls, served at Dorchester Heights, and at 87 rode to Ohio, where he died in 1833. (National Register nomination, Tufts House, NRIS 79000130, section 8, p. 3 of the PDF)

The birth year, Medford origin, Dorchester Heights service, Farmington Falls mills and 1833 death in Ohio all match Rev. Francis Tufts (1744–1833), so the house is now linked to his profile.

⚑ Unverified

Two points in the nomination differ from or go beyond his profile: it places his death at Maineville, Ohio (the family record gives Yankeetown, Ohio), and it says he took part in Arnold’s Quebec Expedition, which no service record on this site yet confirms. It also says “on horseback,” where the family record says by wagon.

The Mystic River

The Mystic runs through the family’s whole New England story. The first Peter kept its ferry. His descendants dug clay along its banks, fished it, milled on its creeks, and farmed the slopes above it in Medford, Charlestown and Malden.

Tufts association: Every association below is cited; see Businesses for the full entries.

  • Ferry (Penny Ferry): Peter Tufts kept the Charlestown–Malden ferry in 1646–47 (Booth, part 1, p. 21), succeeding Philip Drinker in early 1647 (Corey, History of Malden, p. 85, n. 53). Corey calls it the Penny Ferry, superseded by Malden Bridge in 1787; the name “Tufts Landing” is not found in any source checked. Entry · Profile
  • Brickyards on the south bank: the “Sodom” yards of Isaac, Jonathan and Ebenezer Tufts, then Seth Tufts and his son, “near Middlesex Canal and the river” (Brooks, p. 356). Entry
  • Brickyards on the north side: the Fountain Yards of William, Hutchinson and Benjamin Tufts (Brooks, p. 356), and Dr. Simon Tufts’s Brick-yard Pasture (Brooks, p. 355).
  • Fishing: Jonathan Tufts held fishing rights in 1803, and “Isaac Tufts fished from the Bridge to Rock Hill.” One fishing district ran “from the bridge to the beach opposite James Tufts's barn” (Brooks, p. 385). Entry
  • Mills: Dr. Simon Tufts was a party to the 1746 Sandy Bank tide-mill agreement on Gravelly Creek (Brooks, p. 394). William and Edward Tufts owned mills on the Medford Turnpike (Tide Mill Institute compilation).
  • Medford bank homes: the Peter Tufts House on Riverside Avenue (Homesteads), and the Royall farm, where Peter Tufts (b. 1753) settled early (Booth, part 2, p. 22).
  • Charlestown bank: Peter Tufts Jr.’s 1818 Plan of Charlestown Peninsula maps the river frontage (Maps).

⚑ Unverified

No Tufts shipyard on the Mystic has been found. The location of “James Tufts's barn” is not identified.

Maps

Two of the earliest surviving plans of this ground were drawn by a member of the family. Both carry the signature of a “Peter Tufts Junr”: a town plan of Medford made for the Commonwealth in 1794, and a printed plan of the Charlestown peninsula in 1818. Both originals are described as having no known copyright restrictions, and both are shown here from the holding institutions’ scans.

The surveyor

Tufts association: Both plans were drawn by a Peter Tufts Jr.; the 1818 plan by Peter (1774–1825), surveyor, son of Peter Tufts (1753–1832). (Edward C. Booth, “The Tufts Family in Somerville,” Historic Leaves (Somerville Historical Society), vol. 1 (1902–03), p. 23. Perseus)

“Mr. Tufts was a civil engineer by profession, and among the many Peters is designated as ‘Peter, the surveyor.’ He drew a plan of Charlestown in 1818, and the mass of plans that he left behind him shows how laboriously he was engaged in the surveys of public and private property.”

Edward C. Booth, “The Tufts Family in Somerville,” Historic Leaves (Somerville Historical Society), vol. 1 (1902–03), p. 23, on Peter Tufts Jr., born 1774, son of Peter Tufts (1753–1832). Text (Perseus)

Booth adds on the same page that this Peter (1774–1825) was keeper of the Powder House, moved with the powder to the new magazine at Cambridgeport in 1815, and died there in 1825.

⚑ Unverified

Which Peter Tufts signed the 1794 Medford plan. In 1794 both Peter Tufts (1753–1832), then living in Medford, and his son Peter (1774–1825), the surveyor, could have been styled “Junr.” Booth credits the son with the 1818 Charlestown plan only. The attribution of the 1794 plan to the son is likely but not confirmed.

The Powder House and Revolutionary Landmarks

The Old Powder House, Somerville (Nathan Tufts Park)

The stone tower on Quarry Hill was built as a windmill in the early 1700s. In 1747 the province bought the “stone edifice, formerly a windmill” and a quarter-acre of land and made it a gunpowder magazine. It stood then in Charlestown, close to the Medford line. On September 1, 1774, troops sent by Gov. Thomas Gage removed the provincial powder stored there. Rumor spread that blood had been shed, and thousands of militiamen marched toward Boston and Cambridge. This was the “Powder Alarm.” The magazine served until 1818, when the state sold it. (J. L. Bell, Boston 1775, March 2016; Wikipedia: Powder Alarm, tertiary)

Tufts association: Peter Tufts (1753–1832) bought the farm opposite the Powder House in 1788. His son Peter (1774–1825) was the keeper of the Powder House until 1815. Nathan Tufts, the butcher and tanner, owned “the large farms around the Powder House and Walnut hill.” The magazine and its lot were later Tufts property, and the family gave them to Somerville in 1890 as Nathan Tufts Park. (Edward C. Booth, “The Tufts Family in Somerville,” Historic Leaves (Somerville Historical Society), vol. 1 (1902–03), pp. 21–23. Perseus; SAH Archipedia)

⚑ Unverified

Tufts ownership of the magazine lot itself. The Boston 1775 blog and Wikipedia say the state sold it to “Peter Tufts” in 1818; SAH Archipedia says the Tufts family acquired it in 1822. Neither names which Peter, and the deed has not been seen. Booth confirms Tufts farms around the Powder House, not the purchase of the magazine itself. No Tufts is yet documented in the events of September 1, 1774.

Pictured in the Illustrations below. See Peter Tufts Jr.

Penny Ferry / “Tufts Landing”: the Charlestown–Malden Crossing

Tufts association: Peter Tufts the immigrant succeeded Philip Drinker as ferryman at Charlestown Neck in early 1647. This was the ferry later called the Penny Ferry, superseded in 1787 by Malden Bridge, on the line of today’s Alford Street Bridge. The name “Tufts Landing” is not found in any source checked. (Corey, p. 85 and n. 53; Medford Historical Register, vol. XXI, p. 54)

Profile: Peter Tufts the Immigrant.

“He kept the Ferry between Charlestown and Malden with his brother-in-law, Bridges, in 1646-7”

Edward C. Booth, “The Tufts Family in Somerville,” Historic Leaves (Somerville Historical Society), vol. 1 (1902–03), part 1, p. 21, on Peter Tufts the immigrant. Perseus

The crossing from Charlestown Neck to Malden ran across the mouth of the Mystic. The 17th- and 18th-century charts and plans below show this ground. Peter Tufts Jr.’s own 1794 Medford and 1818 Charlestown plans are in Maps. Today the crossing is roughly where Alford Street Bridge runs (OpenStreetMap, © OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL).

⚑ Unverified

That the ferry Peter Tufts kept in 1646–47 was the later “Penny Ferry,” replaced by Malden Bridge (1787) on the line of today’s Alford Street Bridge, is the usual account, but no source quoted on this site says so yet. None of the three period maps has been checked for a labelled ferry. “Tufts Landing” was not found in any source checked.

Siege of Boston plan (1776)

It shows the siege lines that ran across the Charlestown mainland where the Winter Hill and Milk Row Tufts farms lay. The Tufts Subdivisions of South Medford (Winter Hill)

Much of the Winter Hill neighborhood of South Medford was Tufts land in the nineteenth century. Nathan Tufts (1764–1839), a Charlestown butcher and tanner and a son of Nathan and Mary (Adams) Tufts, bought large holdings, including the Dexter farm, and left them to his nephews.

“Nathan Tufts (1764-1839) was a Charlestown butcher and tanner who purchased significant acreage in the two neighboring towns, valued at nearly $120,000 at the time of his death; of this, 630 acres were in six Medford holdings, valued at $51,000.”

Medford Historical Commission, Inventory Form A, “Edward Street Area” (2022). PDF

Amos Tufts’s sons William (1802–1862) and Edward (1806–1875) and grandsons George F. Tufts (1825–1920), a civil engineer and later bank president, and Alfred Tufts (1829–1882), who surveyed the plans, laid the land out in subdivisions in 1854 and 1861. Joseph, Edward and Henry Streets “were named for members of the Tufts family, Dexter after the earlier owner.” George Tufts reorganized part of section 5 in 1880, and the six end houses of Edward Street were built by 1889 (Medford Historical Commission, Inventory Form A, “Edward Street Area” (2022).
