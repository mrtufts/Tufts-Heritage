Long before the college that bears the name, the Tufts family earned its living from the clay, water and roads of the Mystic valley: ferrying, brickmaking, tanning, milling, fishing, distilling, printing and, later, pharmacy. Each entry below names the family member, the trade, the place and the dates, quoting the printed page. Doubtful identifications are marked Unverified. See also the Mystic River and Homesteads.

The Charlestown–Malden Ferry

Tufts: Peter Tufts (the immigrant, 1617–1700) · Business: Ferry · Location: Mystic River, between Charlestown and Malden · Dates: 1646–47

“He kept the Ferry between Charlestown and Malden with his brother-in-law, Bridges, in 1646-7”

Edward C. Booth, “The Tufts Family in Somerville,” Historic Leaves (Somerville Historical Society), vol. 1 (1902–03), part 1, p. 21. Perseus

The ferry crossed the Mystic. Booth adds that Peter was the largest landholder in Malden when he died in 1700. See Peter Tufts the Immigrant.

⚑ Unverified

The ferry’s exact landing places and the length of his tenure. Booth found no earlier record of him than 1646–47.

Brick-yard Pasture, Medford

Tufts: Dr. Simon Tufts · Business: Brickmaking land · Location: North of Dr. Tufts’s house, Medford · Dates: Bought March 26, 1761

“At a later date, say 1750, bricks were made on land directly north of Dr. Tufts's house. … This land, called Brick-yard Pasture, was owned by Rev. Matthew Byles, of Boston, and sold by him to Dr. Simon Tufts, March 26, 1761.”

Charles Brooks, History of the Town of Medford (1855), p. 355. Text (Perseus)

Dr. Tufts’s house stood at Medford Square (see Homesteads).

⚑ Unverified

Brooks records the purchase of the land, not that Dr. Tufts made bricks himself. By 1761 the elder Dr. Simon had died (1747), so the buyer was presumably the younger Dr. Simon (1726–1786). Not confirmed by deed.

The “Sodom” Brickyards

Tufts: Isaac, Jonathan and Ebenezer Tufts, brothers; later Seth Tufts and his son Seth · Business: Brickmaking · Location: South side of the Mystic, near the Middlesex Canal, south-south-east of Rock Hill (between Summer and George Streets, on Brickyard Lane) · Dates: 18th century to shortly before 1855

“As the familiar and improper sobriquet of Sodom was early given to that part of Medford which lies south of the river, the brick-yards, opened by the brothers Isaac, Jonathan, and Ebenezer Tufts, obtained the local name. After these gentlemen came Seth Tufts, who, with his son Seth, carried on the business till recently. These yards were situated near Middlesex Canal and the river, about south-south-east from Rock Hill.”

Charles Brooks, History of the Town of Medford (1855), p. 356. Text (Perseus)

The street location is from the Medford Historical Society. Medford bricks were carried by lighter down the Mystic to Boston.

Tan-yard and leather works

Tufts: Nathan Tufts · Business: Tanning and butchering; leather manufacture · Location: Medford (corner south-east of Whitmore’s Bridge), later Charlestown · Dates: Before 1855

“The first tan-yard in Medford was on the corner lot south-east of Whitmore's Bridge. … It was last owned by Mr. Nathan Tufts and Mr. Jonathan Brooks, in company. When they sold it, Mr. Tufts moved to Charlestown, and became the most extensive manufacturer of leather in the State.”

Charles Brooks, History of the Town of Medford (1855), p. 353. Text (Perseus)

“Nathan, the youngest son of Nathan, Sr., was also a resident of Charlestown after his boyhood, and was an extensive butcher and tanner there.”

Edward C. Booth, “The Tufts Family in Somerville,” Historic Leaves (Somerville Historical Society), vol. 1 (1902–03), part 2, p. 21. Perseus

Booth adds that he owned “the large farms around the Powder House and Walnut hill” (p. 21). See the Powder House. Sawyer’s account of this Charlestown butcher and tanner, who died in October 1835 aged seventy-one, includes the tide mill and mill pond at the Neck. See Tide mill of Nathan Tufts and his profile.

⚑ Unverified

That Brooks’s Medford tan-yard partner and Booth’s and Sawyer’s Charlestown butcher and tanner are the same Nathan Tufts. The match is likely, but Sawyer does not name the Medford yard.

Tide mill of Nathan Tufts, Charlestown

Tufts: Nathan Tufts (c. 1764–October 1835) · Business: Tide mill (grist); mill pond; tannery and lumber wharf · Location: Tufts Mill Pond, Mystic River side of Charlestown Neck, just below present Sullivan Square (approx. 42.3864° N, 71.0717° W) · Dates: Built in Nathan Tufts’s lifetime (he died 1835); pond and an abutting tannery acquired by the city in 1891 and filled by 1905

A lantern slide in the Boston Public Library’s Charlestown collection, titled “Tide mill of Nathan Tufts,” shows a cluster of wooden, shingled mill buildings on an unpaved lot. Across the gable of the nearer building a painted sign reads “NATHAN TUFTS & SON.”

The mill belonged to Nathan Tufts, brother of Daniel Tufts and Deacon Amos Tufts, and son of Nathan Tufts, a cordwainer and Medford farmer.

“One of the sons of this Peter was Nathan Tufts, by trade a cordwainer, but chiefly occupied as a farmer in Medford. He was the father of Daniel, Deacon Amos, and Nathan Tufts, who for the greater part of their lives were prominent citizens of the old town and among its leading business men.”

Timothy T. Sawyer, Old Charlestown: Historical, Biographical, Reminiscent (Boston, 1902), p. 162. Internet Archive

The younger Nathan, Sawyer wrote, “was a noted man in the old town.” He began as a butcher and packed beef on a large scale, then became widely known for the quality of the sole-leather he tanned. He kept a wharf on the Mystic-river side of Charlestown, dealing in wood and lumber, “and a grist-mill made a part of his establishment.”

“His tannery and wharf were on the Mystic-river side of the town, and a good many of the town's people found steady employment there. Besides this, he built the Mill-pond at the Neck, and was connected with Abel Fitz in the grain and meal business there. He also started a new tannery there, and carried it on as long as he lived. It was afterwards sold to Chester Guild & Sons”

Timothy T. Sawyer, Old Charlestown: Historical, Biographical, Reminiscent (Boston, 1902), p. 167–168. Internet Archive

In another passage Sawyer places that pond “by the side of Maiden Bridge,” with “the mills … grinding, slowly perhaps, but surely” (Timothy T. Sawyer, Old Charlestown: Historical, Biographical, Reminiscent (Boston, 1902), p. 463. Internet Archive). The 1848 city plan labels the rectangular basin on the Mystic shore “Tufts Mill Pond,” next to the bridge, and shows the earlier Charlestown Mill Pond separately, south of the Neck. The Medford Historical Register later called Nathan’s works “a tide-mill just below Sullivan square.” “Why Mystic?,” The Medford Historical Register, vol. XXI, no. 3 (July 1918), p. 53. Volume scan (Internet Archive)

The two ponds are easily confused. On a map of the Revolutionary period, the Register noted, “the area now covered by railroad tracks, freight houses etc., [was] the Charlestown Mill Pond. A later map would show the Tufts’ Mill Pond, where is now the Charlestown Playground.” “The Second Battle of Bunker’s Hill,” The Medford Historical Register, vol. XI, no. 2 (April 1908), p. 43. Volume scan (Internet Archive) It was the older dam, not Nathan’s later pond, that Major Knowlton and about a hundred men crossed on the night of January 8, 1776, “the Mill Dam which lays between Cobble Hill and Bunker’s Hill,” in a raid on houses still standing outside the burned town. The New-England Chronicle printed the account on January 11, 1776, quoted in the same Register article (pp. 43–44).

Nathan lived in the house on Chelsea Street first built and occupied by Aaron Putnam, later known as the home of Dr. Lambert (Timothy T. Sawyer, Old Charlestown: Historical, Biographical, Reminiscent (Boston, 1902), p. 168, 171. Internet Archive). He died in October 1835, aged seventy-one, and had no children. By his will, after provision for his wife—his widow Mary later testified in a suit over the estate—and a few bequests, among them $1,000 to the Bunker Hill Monument Association, the bulk of his property went to his nephews and nieces, the children of Daniel and Deacon Amos (Timothy T. Sawyer, Old Charlestown: Historical, Biographical, Reminiscent (Boston, 1902), p. 168. Internet Archive). Among those heirs on this site are William Tufts (1802–1862), Edward Tufts (1806–1875), and the next generation, George F. Tufts (1825–1920) and Alfred Tufts (1829–1882). See also the tan-yard and leather works and the homestead note on Places.

A Tide Mill Institute compilation, citing Seasholes, states that the city acquired Tufts Mill Pond and an abutting tannery in 1891 and finished filling the site in 1905, making Charlestown Playground, renamed the John J. Ryan, Jr., Playground in 1942. The same compilation gives the approximate location 42.3864° N, 71.0717° W, near Sullivan Square. Tide Mill Institute, “MA Charlestown, Tufts Mill.” PDF

⚑ Unverified

The sign “NATHAN TUFTS & SON” cannot be read as naming a child of this Nathan, who, Sawyer says, had no children (p. 168). A later firm, Nathan Tufts & Sons, grain dealers, occupied a Warren Avenue wharf and store as successors to Wesson & Gary (Sawyer, p. 82); that Nathan, accidentally killed October 20, 1887, was the son of a different Nathan Tufts (Daniel’s son, the brick-maker, who died August 1, 1872) (Sawyer, p. 163). Whether the mill sign names a relative, a successor, or a trading style is not established. The lantern slide is dated only ca. 1850–1925. The 1891 acquisition, 1905 filling, and 1942 renaming rest on the Tide Mill Institute compilation citing Seasholes; the original Seasholes page has not been checked. Claims that the earlier Charlestown mills date from the 1640s, had a pond of about thirty acres, that the 1776 raid was ordered by General Washington, or that those older mills were later tied to the Middlesex Canal, have not been verified in the sources used here. (The 1848 plan does show the Middlesex Canal immediately west of Tufts Mill Pond.)

Spot Pond mill and dam, Malden, 1794–1800

Tufts association. Samuel Tufts owned “a certain mill, mill-dam, and mill-site, situated on a certain brook there, called Spot-Pond Brook, in Malden,” the brook that flows out of Spot Pond in Stoneham. In his plea he called it “an ancient mill and mill-dam, and mill privilege,” held as his own estate in fee simple on February 17, 1794, and kept up by him for more than six years. John Buckman, whose sixteen acres the dam flooded, sued for damages. On appeal the Supreme Judicial Court found that “Samuel Tufts the defendant, has had, and now has, a prescriptive right to keep up the dam, in the same situation and height, as in his plea he has declared.”

John Buckman v. Samuel Tufts, Supreme Judicial Court, in James Sullivan, The History of Land Titles in Massachusetts (Boston, 1801), pp. 280–282. Google Books. See also “Medford’s Water Supply,” The Medford Historical Register, vol. XIII, no. 3 (July 1910), p. 61. Volume scan (Internet Archive)

Edmund Tufts, Printer

Tufts: Edmund Tufts, son of Joseph Tufts (1760–1819) · Business: Printing · Location: The Peter Tufts of Winter Hill homestead, Somerville · Dates: About 1842–1851 and after

“Edmund was intimately connected with the early history of this town, and his sign on the old house, ‘Edmund Tufts, Printer,’ is still remembered. For some years he did the printing for the new town of Somerville and its inhabitants, and we find his name on most of the early town reports. He issued a Somerville Directory in 1851, a pamphlet of thirty-two pages.”

James W. Tufts, apothecary and soda-fountain maker

Tufts: James Walker Tufts (1835–1902), son of Leonard Tufts of Charlestown · Business: Pharmacy and drug stores; Arctic soda apparatus; silver-plated ware · Location: Somerville Avenue near the Bleachery; Medford; Boston (Washington and Hanover Streets) · Dates: 1856–1895

“Leonard, who lived in Charlestown, was the father of James W. Tufts, who was at one time an apothecary in Somerville avenue, near the Bleachery. Mr. Tufts has since become well-known as a manufacturer of soda-water apparatus.”

Edward C. Booth, “The Tufts Family in Somerville,” Historic Leaves (Somerville Historical Society), vol. 1 (1902–03), part 2, p. 24. Perseus

He was apprenticed at fifteen to Samuel Kidder and Company, druggists of Charlestown, and opened his own pharmacy in Somerville in 1856. He later had stores in Medford, Boston, Winchester and Woburn, then sold the stores to make his Arctic Soda Apparatus. In 1891 his firm merged into the American Soda Fountain Company, which he headed. He also built the Bunker Hill Terraces tenements in Charlestown (1890) and later founded Pinehurst, N.C. (NCpedia) From 1875 his firm also made silver-plated wares. (Smithsonian) Booth places him in the line of John Tufts (b. 1755) of the Peter and Oliver Tufts House.

⚑ Unverified

NCpedia gives his apprenticeship at fifteen; another account says sixteen.

Tufts Express & Van Co., Chicago

Tufts: William Tufts (b. about 1888) and family; O. J. Tufts, manager (1925) · Business: Express, moving and household-goods storage; later furniture sales · Location: 4341 W. Madison St., later also 4444 W. Madison St., West Side of Chicago · Dates: About 1900/1902 to at least 1933

On the far West Side of Chicago, a schoolboy who would not stay in school built a moving business from the family horse and a sawed-off lunch car. The Chicago Daily Tribune told his story in 1908:

“Today, at 20 years old, William Tufts is a west side business man, head of the establishment, with his father and brother working for him.”

Hollis W. Field, “Plays Hookey and Succeeds,” Chicago Daily Tribune, Aug. 23, 1908, Sunday magazine. Internet Archive

“That was the real beginning of the Tufts express and van business … There are ten horses and eleven wagons in the Tufts establishment now”

Same article

The article dates the start to “about 1902,” on “the eastern edge of Austin,” and says he had just bought three auto trucks for $3,000 apiece. The 1911 Lakeside directory lists a Tufts at “4341 W Madison” among Chicago expressmen (Lakeside Annual Directory, 1911). The trade journal Distribution & Warehousing lists “Tufts Express, 4341 West Madison. Estd. 1900.” (Dec. 1920, Internet Archive) In January 1925 it announced that the firm “will soon enter the furniture warehousing business” in a six-story fireproof building on West Madison Street. Its listing that month reads “Tufts Express & Van Co., 4341 W. Madison. O. J. Tufts, Mgr.” (Internet Archive)

From 1926 the Tribune’s classified pages carry its ads at the new address. One reads “TUFT’S FIREPROOF & VAN Co., 4444 W. MADISON-ST.” (Apr. 12, 1929, Internet Archive) Another, “Can be seen at Tufts Storage, 4444 W. Madison. Open evenings and Sunday”, comes from the 1932 furniture-sale ads (Feb. 5, 1932, Internet Archive). The ads continue into 1933.
