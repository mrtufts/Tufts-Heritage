A Shield Borrowed Across the Sea

Many New England families of the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries hung a painted or embroidered coat of arms in the parlor, lettered beneath with the words “By the Name of” and the family surname. The Tufts family was no exception. The design that has come down under the Tufts name, though, was never granted to anyone called Tufts. Its shield is the coat of an English family named Tufnell, borrowed because the names sounded alike. This page sets out what the records show about that shield, where the American custom came from, and the other arms that have been linked to the name.

The achievement, as shown in the reproduction, has these parts:

  • Shield: blue (azure), crossed by a broad white horizontal band (a fess argent) bearing three black birds (martlets sable). There are three white ostrich feathers on the blue, two above the band and one below.
  • Helmet and mantling: a steel helmet in profile above the shield, with leafy mantling in red, gold and cream on either side.
  • Crest: a brown animal’s head, lion-like, rising from a twisted wreath of gold and blue on the helmet.
  • Behind the shield: two crossed green sprays or feathers.
  • Ribbon: a pink scroll lettered “BY THE NAME OF TUFTS.”

In formal heraldry a martlet is drawn without feet. The birds in the reproduction have feet, as many American folk renderings did.

The Tufnell Arms

The shield matches, charge for charge and colour for colour, the arms that Burke’s General Armory records for the Tufnell family of Langleys, Essex:

“Tufnell (Langleys, co. Essex; descended from Richard Tufnaile, or Tufnell, citizen and brewer of London, M.P. for Southwark in 1640. John Jolliffe Tufnell, Esq., of Langleys, was High Sheriff co. Essex 1823). Az. on a fess betw. three ostrich feathers ar. as many martlets sa. Crest—A dexter arm embowed in armour ppr. holding in the gauntlet a cutlass ar. hilt or.”

Sir Bernard Burke, The General Armory of England, Scotland, Ireland, and Wales (London, 1884), p. 1035. Book scan (Internet Archive) (Scanned text; obvious scanning errors corrected.)

In plain words: a blue shield; a silver band between three silver ostrich feathers; on the band, three black martlets. Burke gives the Tufnell crest as an arm in armour, bent at the elbow, holding a cutlass. The animal-head crest in the American painting is therefore not the Tufnell crest. It may be the painter’s choice or a crest borrowed from another book. Its source has not been identified.

Burke has no entry at all under “Tufts.” The neighbouring entries on the same page are for unrelated names: Tuffle (“Gu. six ostrich feathers ar.”) and the Tufton Earls of Thanet ((“Sa. an eagle displ. erm.” and variants) (Burke, p. 1035)ldquo;Sa. an eagle displ. erm. a border ar.(“Sa. an eagle displ. erm.” and variants) (Burke, p. 1035)rdquo;) (Burke, pp. 1035–36).

“By the Name of”: The American Custom

Very few colonial families held a grant of arms from the English College of Arms, but arms still carried prestige. Historic Deerfield’s catalogue explains how families chose them:

“Although few American families were conferred arms by the English College of Heralds, the aristocratic connotation was no less meaningful to established families in New England; they did not hesitate to select arms of people with common surnames. By 1730, Boston heraldic painters had access to a number of publications that illustrated coats of arms from which to copy or combine elements.”

Historic Deerfield, “needlework coat of arms: Tufts,” ca. 1800, Hartford, Conn., accession no. HD 1998. Museum record (Five College Museums)

Boston painters made many of these pictures. Historic Deerfield holds several watercolours lettered “By the Name of” that it attributes to John Coles Sr. or his son John Coles Jr., among them Putnam, Jones and Hurd, all dated about 1800 (Historic Deerfield, “Coat of Arms: By the Name of Putnam,” possibly John Coles Sr. or John Coles Jr., ca. 1800, accession no. HD 2019.16. Museum record; see also “By the Name of Jones” (HD 2431.1) and “By the Name of Hurd” (HD 2431.2), same attribution.). The Tufts painting fits this pattern: an English shield found in a book under a similar name, a helmet and mantling, and the surname on a ribbon. No Coles painting for Tufts has been found.

The “Tuffts Family” Embroidery at Historic Deerfield

The only Tufts coat of arms in a museum found so far is a needlework piece at Historic Deerfield, catalogued as “needlework coat of arms: Tufts,” ca. 1800, made in Hartford, Connecticut. It is polychrome silk embroidery on a white satin silk ground, accession number HD 1998. The museum describes it as an “Embroidered silk coat-of-arms of the Tuffts Family” (Historic Deerfield, “needlework coat of arms: Tufts,” ca. 1800, Hartford, Conn., accession no. HD 1998. What the American Armories Record

Bolton’s American Armory (1927) lists arms actually used in America, from seals, gravestones, bookplates and silver. It has no entry for Tufts. Its entries pass from Tufton to Tuite (p. 167). The Tufton entry is the seal of John Tufton, Sheriff of New Hampshire, on a document of August 1688. Tufton is a different family from the Charlestown and Medford Tufts.

The name does turn up in Bolton under other families’ arms. The Norton arms were “Engr. on tankard given by Mrs. Elizabeth Quincy, daughter of Rev. John Norton of Hingham, Mass., to her daughter, Lucy Tufts. In First Ch. Quincy, Mass.” (Bolton, Norton entry, pp. 118–124). Lucy (Quincy) Tufts was the first wife of Dr. Cotton Tufts. Bolton also records a framed watercolour of the Whitney arms by Mrs. Mary Lovering Holman, “Owned by Mrs. Susan Cotton Tufts, Brookline, Mass.” (Bolton, p. 180).

Crozier’s General Armory (1904), a registry of American families “entitled to coat armor,” has no Tufts entry either (William Armstrong Crozier, Crozier’s General Armory: A Registry of American Families Entitled to Coat Armor (New York, 1904). Book scan (Internet Archive); searched in the full text).

Tufts College: Seal and Coat of Arms

Tufts College, named for Charles Tufts, who gave its hilltop land, has its own heraldry. It has nothing to do with the family painting. The trustees adopted the college seal on July 17, 1857. It shows a dove with an olive branch flying down toward an open Bible on a rocky height, with the motto “Pax et Lux” (“Peace and Light”) (Anne Sauer et al., Concise Encyclopedia of Tufts History (Tufts University, 2000), “College Seal, 1857.” Text (Tufts Digital Library)).

In 1939 President Leonard Carmichael introduced a separate coat of arms for everyday use, designed by Maurice Biscoe of the architects Andrews, Jones, Biscoe and Whitmore. It was a blue shield quartered with the sun, two books and the descending dove, with the motto on a ribbon. Brown, one of the college colours, was left out “because brown was not an appropriate color in heraldry.” The arms were dropped in the mid-1960s, and an updated seal reading “Sigillum Universitatis Tuftensis” was adopted in 1966 (Anne Sauer et al., Concise Encyclopedia of Tufts History (Tufts University, 2000), “Coat of Arms, 1939-66.” Text (Tufts Digital Library)).

Commercial “Tufts Crest” Claims

Several online shops sell a “Tufts family crest” on rings, prints and mugs, among them crestsandarms.com and heraldicjewelry.com. They give no source for the arms they show, and some show designs unlike the painting. These are commercial claims. They are not evidence that any Tufts was granted arms, and they are not used as sources on this site.

In Short

The “By the Name of Tufts” arms are a piece of American family art. The shield is the Tufnell coat as recorded by Burke, adopted for the Tufts name by the New England custom of borrowing arms from a family with a similar surname. No grant of arms to anyone named Tufts has been found in Burke, Bolton or Crozier. The arms are still part of the family’s story. They show how a Charlestown and Medford family of farmers, doctors and tradesmen chose to present itself around 1800.
